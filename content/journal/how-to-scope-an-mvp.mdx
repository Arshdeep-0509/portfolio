First products tend to go wrong in one of two directions. Some attempt everything, spend a year in
development and open to an empty room. Others trim so hard that the result solves nothing, and early
users drift away before the founder has learned anything. Good scoping lands in between: small
enough to ship within weeks, whole enough that someone who has the problem would genuinely use it.

"Minimum" is the word that trips people up. It doesn't mean the fewest features you can get away
with. It means the smallest build that still delivers the central value to one clearly defined group.
A booking tool that cannot take a booking is a demo, not an MVP. A booking tool for a single kind of
clinic — no reports, no integrations, a plain admin screen — could be exactly right.

Investors judge an MVP much as users do. A long feature list doesn't move them. They look for proof
that you understood a real problem, made clear decisions about what to leave out, and gathered
evidence — sign-ups, usage, retention, payments — from what you released. A modest product with
visible traction makes a far stronger case than a large one nobody opens.

Below is the scoping routine I use with founders: begin with the riskiest assumption, trace one core
journey from start to finish, cut features with a single question, and agree what "done" means
before any code exists. A short checklist at the end helps you test your scope before money is spent.
