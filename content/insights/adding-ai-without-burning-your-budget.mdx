Almost every founder I speak to is under some pressure to "add AI". Sometimes the pressure comes from
investors, sometimes from competitors, sometimes from a genuinely good idea. The risk is the same in
each case: spending weeks and a surprising amount of money on a feature that demos well, answers
confidently, and turns out to be wrong just often enough that nobody trusts it.

AI features that pay off usually share three traits. They target a narrow, repetitive task where a
right answer exists — answering common support questions, pulling fields out of invoices, finding
the relevant paragraph in a pile of documents. They're grounded in your own data rather than the
model's general knowledge. And they're measured before launch, against real examples from your
business, so you know how often they're right instead of hoping.

Cost is the other half of the story. Model pricing is per token, which makes it easy to ignore while
you're testing and easy to be surprised by in production. The good news is that most of the cost is
controllable: choosing a smaller model when it's good enough, sending less text, caching repeated
work, and putting hard limits in place before real users arrive.

In this post I'll share the process I use: finding a use case worth automating, building a cheap
prototype in a week or two, scoring it with an evaluation set, and the handful of engineering habits
that keep a monthly AI bill boring. None of it requires a research team — just the same discipline
you'd apply to any other feature.

## Keep costs predictable

<Callout type="warning">
  Set spending caps and alerts with your model provider before launch, not after the first invoice.
</Callout>

A simple guard in code helps too:

```ts
// Refuse requests that would push today's spend over the daily budget.
export async function withinBudget(estimatedCost: number) {
  const spentToday = await getSpendForToday(); // from your usage log
  return spentToday + estimatedCost <= DAILY_AI_BUDGET;
}
```
