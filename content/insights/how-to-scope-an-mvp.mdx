Most first products fail in one of two ways. Some try to do everything, take a year to build and
launch into silence. Others cut so deep that what ships doesn't solve anything, and early users
leave before the founder learns a thing. A well-scoped MVP sits between those extremes: small enough
to launch in weeks, complete enough that someone with the problem would actually use it.

The word "minimum" is where founders go wrong. It doesn't mean "as little as possible". It means
the least you can build that still delivers the core value to one specific group of people. A
booking tool that can't take a booking isn't an MVP; it's a demo. A booking tool for one type of
clinic, with no reporting, no integrations and a simple admin screen, might be exactly right.

Investors read MVPs the same way users do. They aren't impressed by a long feature list. They want
to see that you understood a real problem, made sharp choices about what to leave out, and got
evidence — sign-ups, usage, retention, payments — from the version you shipped. A small product
with clear traction tells a far better story than a large one nobody uses.

In this guide I'll walk through the scoping process I use with founders: starting from the riskiest
assumption, mapping one core journey end to end, cutting features with a simple test, and deciding
what "done" means before a line of code is written. You'll also get a short checklist to sanity-check
your scope before you commit budget to it.
